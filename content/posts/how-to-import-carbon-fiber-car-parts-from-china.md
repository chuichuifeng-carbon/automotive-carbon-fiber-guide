**Plan the product, acceptance process and import responsibilities before paying for production.** For automotive carbon fiber parts, vehicle fitment and packaging can matter as much as unit price. Customs classification and charges depend on the actual article, origin, destination and entry date; a material name alone is not enough to determine them.

## A 12-step import workflow

| Step | Buyer action | Record to retain |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Define the application | Identify vehicle configuration, part, construction and destination market | SKU specification and intended use |
| 2. Verify the seller | Reconcile seller, manufacturing role and payment beneficiary | Quotation and supplier verification record |
| 3. Compare quotations | Use identical quantities, packing and delivery scope | Cost comparison with exclusions |
| 4. Review import requirements | Ask a destination customs broker about classification, valuation and applicable requirements | Broker advice for the exact article |
| 5. Evaluate the sample | Inspect fitment, finish, kit contents and packaging | Approved sample revision and open issues |
| 6. Agree commercial terms | Confirm milestones, inspection, remedies and delivery responsibilities | Purchase order or contract |
| 7. Release production | Approve the specification and authorized quantity | Written production release |
| 8. Check the batch | Inspect against the agreed plan before dispatch | Inspection report and corrective actions |
| 9. Approve documents | Reconcile invoice, packing list, transport and origin information as applicable | Final shipping document set |
| 10. Dispatch and monitor | Confirm booking, route, insurance and handover | Carrier/forwarder references |
| 11. Clear the shipment | Work with the appointed broker and responsible importer | Entry records and charges |
| 12. Inspect receipt | Record carton condition, counts and product discrepancies | Receiving and claims evidence |

Ask for order-specific dates at each milestone. Production, booking and clearance estimates are separate; none should be treated as a guaranteed total transit time without written agreement.

## Customs codes and duty: avoid a generic lookup shortcut

A finished vehicle-specific component, raw fabric and a composite sheet are different articles. Ask your broker to review the product description, function, construction, photographs, origin and destination tariff schedule. Do not reuse a supplier's export code as an automatic destination import classification.

For US shipments, [CBP explains why classification is needed to determine import requirements](https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1397). Use the [US International Trade Commission's HTS](https://hts.usitc.gov/) for the applicable tariff schedule and obtain advice on any additional measures. For EU shipments, consult the [European Commission's TARIC database](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/online-services/online-services-and-databases-customs/eu-customs-tariff-taric_en).

Keep customs value separate from your internal landed-cost calculation. [CBP's invoice-value guidance](https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1162?language=en_US) explains that US duty is generally assessed on the price paid for the goods rather than international freight and insurance, subject to the applicable valuation basis. Have the broker confirm your transaction's treatment.

## Compare delivery quotations on the same basis

Record the named place and agreed Incoterms version. Ask who arranges transport, pays destination charges, handles import clearance and bears risk at each handover. For a DDP offer, confirm the importer arrangements and what evidence of clearance will be available. Convenience alone does not establish compliance or the lowest total cost.

Do not compare an ex-factory offer with a delivered offer without reconciling their scope. Ask the forwarder about chargeable weight, oversize fees, consolidation, storage and last-mile access for large parts.

## Build a landed-cost worksheet

Include goods, tooling allocation, export packing, inspection, origin handling, international freight, insurance, destination charges, broker fees, applicable duties/taxes, delivery and a realistic damage/returns allowance. Identify recoverable taxes separately with your accountant.

**Illustrative calculation, not a tariff or freight quote:** goods $4,000 + packing/inspection $300 + transport/handling $900 + broker-confirmed import charges $600 + final delivery $200 = $6,000 cash outlay. If 20 units are saleable, that is $300 per saleable unit before selling expenses. Replace every input with your own quotation.

## Receiving and claims checklist

- Photograph outer packaging and labels before opening.
- Count components and hardware against the packing list.
- Preserve damaged packaging and isolate affected stock.
- Record SKU, batch, revision and the actual discrepancy.
- Notify the relevant parties within the agreed carrier, insurance and supplier deadlines.

There is no universal 24-hour claim rule for every shipment. Obtain the applicable deadlines before dispatch.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do all carbon fiber car parts use the same HS code?

No. Classification depends on the actual article and destination rules. Obtain a broker's assessment or an appropriate customs ruling where needed.

### Should a first order be a full production batch?

Qualify the [sample]({{< relref "posts/carbon-fiber-sample-evaluation.md" >}}) first, then choose a trial quantity that fits your cash exposure and verified demand. Confirm [SKU-level MOQ]({{< relref "posts/carbon-fiber-moq-guide.md" >}}).

### Is the supplier's inspection a product approval for my market?

No. Confirm applicable product, installation and market requirements separately. Inspection checks the agreed scope; it does not create regulatory approval.

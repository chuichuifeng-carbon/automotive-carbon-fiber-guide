**A reference photo can start a carbon fiber development discussion, but it cannot supply every dimension, mounting interface or manufacturing requirement.** Describe what you want to achieve, identify the actual vehicle and distinguish visible styling from unknown geometry. Ask for a feasibility review and the next required inputs before treating an initial estimate as a tooling or production commitment.

For an aftermarket brand, this distinction can save several rounds of misleading quotations. The objective is not to make the first email look technically complete. It is to make the important unknowns visible so the manufacturer can propose a meaningful next step.

## Start by explaining the decision you need

“Can you make this?” can mean several different things: finding a similar catalog item, adapting an existing product, manufacturing a buyer-owned design or developing new geometry. A supplier cannot reliably choose the route from an image alone.

Write one sentence about the intended outcome. For example: “We want to review a new cosmetic lower-bumper component for a specified vehicle, using the attached image only as an appearance reference.” That identifies the starting point without claiming the photo provides a manufacturable design.

CCF's guide to [identifying carbon fiber parts from customer photographs](https://ccfrp.com/identify-carbon-fiber-parts-customer-photos/) explains why visual similarity is not the same as confirmed part identity. Apply the same caution when moving from identification into development.

## Separate what the image shows from what it leaves unknown

Use an evidence table rather than drawing confident conclusions from a single view.

| Topic | A photo may help communicate | What still needs confirmation |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | General outline, visible surface and styling intention | Exact sections, transitions and appearance acceptance |
| Application | A vehicle family or a proposed installation location | Actual bumper, body style, production configuration and modifications |
| Geometry | Relative relationships in the visible view | Dimensions, hidden surfaces and reference coordinates |
| Mounting | Visible supports or exposed attachment areas | Fixing design, load paths, clearances and supplied hardware |
| Construction | The appearance of a visible surface | Reinforcement, backing, resin, process and laminate specification |
| Supply scope | Components you want to discuss | Parts included in development and production |

Do not infer the manufacturing process from gloss or weave appearance. Likewise, a component pictured on a car does not prove how it is mounted, whether it fits another configuration or whether the visible installation is an approved final design.

Mark each field as supplied, proposed or unknown. This is more useful than filling the blanks with assumptions that later become embedded in a price.

## Prepare an annotated reference set

Number each image and explain what you want the reviewer to notice. “Image 01: overall styling direction; Image 02: preferred outer corner shape” is more precise than attaching twelve files with no explanation.

If you own the vehicle or have access through a customer, add photographs of the actual installation area. Include wider views showing adjacent equipment and closer views of relevant interfaces. Do not use a ruler somewhere in the image as an automatic substitute for controlled measurement: perspective and the chosen reference can make a casual image unsuitable for determining geometry.

Keep desired features separate from features you do not want copied. A brand may like a general profile but need different mounting, clearance or kit content. State those differences explicitly.

Also explain the reference's origin and the rights you have to use it. An online photograph does not establish permission to reproduce another party's design. For commercially significant design-rights questions, obtain the appropriate professional review rather than assuming the manufacturer can resolve them from your email.

## Record the interfaces that control the project

The visible face can dominate the conversation while the hidden installation surfaces remain undefined. Ask your technical or installation reviewer which interfaces must be known before tooling can be authorized.

These may include mating surfaces, mounting locations, moving components, sensors, cooling paths or service access. Their relevance depends on the part. A decorative cover and a load-bearing assembly should not be reviewed as if they had the same requirements.

Use CCF's [custom carbon fiber feasibility brief](https://ccfrp.com/custom-carbon-fiber-feasibility-brief/) to organize the intended application and unresolved requirements. The next output should identify what information is missing, who can obtain it and which decision it affects.

If a supplier proposes a physical reference, vehicle measurements, CAD or scan data, ask what scope is needed. Collecting a large quantity of data is not necessarily useful if the critical installation interface is absent.

## When scan data becomes part of the plan

A scan can support geometry review, but the file still needs context. Identify the scanned object, its condition, the coordinate reference, units, relevant areas and any limitations reported by the data provider. Keep data revisions traceable to the physical configuration.

CCF's guide to [preparing 3D scan data for a parts RFQ](https://ccfrp.com/3d-scan-data-carbon-fiber-parts-manufacturing/) is a useful next-stage reference. Ask the receiving team about formats and required coverage before paying for data collection.

Do not promise a supplier a usable engineering file when you only have a visual mesh or an unverified export. Describe what the file actually represents and let the review identify whether further work is needed.

## Worked example: a useful brief with incomplete information

The following is an invented teaching example, not a CCF project or engineering recommendation.

A small brand wants a new front-lip appearance for a particular customer vehicle. It has a front three-quarter photograph, a styling sketch and access to a car for one afternoon. It has no dimensions or underside data.

An unhelpful request would ask for a final unit price and delivery date based on those attachments. A more useful brief separates the available inputs from the proposed work:

| Brief section | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Intended result | Review a new front-lip styling concept for the specified configuration |
| Available references | One appearance photo and one buyer-created sketch |
| Confirmed application | Vehicle configuration supplied separately; bumper photos attached |
| Unconfirmed interfaces | Underside mounting, clearances and any required modifications |
| Verification resource | Buyer can arrange a limited vehicle session after agreeing its scope |
| Requested next step | Feasibility discussion and an input/verification plan |
| Commercial boundary | No tooling or production release requested yet |

The useful answer is not necessarily a production quotation. It may be a request for interface data, an existing catalog alternative or a staged development proposal. Each can move the project forward if the assumptions and next decisions are explicit.

The buyer should record what was learned and what remains unresolved. If the vehicle session cannot capture the necessary interfaces, that limitation belongs in the plan before anybody commits to tooling.

## Plan verification before promising a production date

Identify where the sample will be checked, which vehicle configuration will be available and who will inspect it. Agree what happens if an interface needs correction or the required vehicle is unavailable.

The [test-fit vehicle session guide](https://ccfrp.com/carbon-fiber-prototype-test-fit-vehicle/) helps turn general access to a car into a defined review opportunity. A short window should have an agreed priority list rather than an expectation that every performance, durability and installation question will be answered at once.

## A first email you can adapt

Before asking for a deadline, identify the event from which that deadline would run. An initial discussion, a complete input set, a tooling release and sample approval are different milestones. If a commercial launch depends on development, record which assumptions must hold for the proposed schedule and which reviews still need time. Do not remove an unresolved verification stage merely to fit a launch date. Ask for a staged plan whose dependencies your team can support; an early estimate should remain an estimate until the relevant scope and responsibilities are settled.

> We are exploring [component] for [vehicle configuration and intended use]. The attached photographs communicate [specific styling features]; they are not dimensional drawings. We currently have [available references] and can arrange [vehicle/sample/data access]. Unknowns include [interfaces and requirements]. Please review whether an existing product or a development route may be suitable, identify the next inputs needed and separate feasibility work from tooling, prototype and production decisions.

Before transferring sensitive files, discuss the appropriate confidentiality arrangements. Keep the reference list and revision names in the email so later discussions refer to the same information.

## Questions to close after the initial discussion

Who owns each input and obtains missing information? What is the next deliverable? Which assumptions must be confirmed before a useful price can be prepared? What work is authorized now, and what is explicitly waiting for another decision?

You do not need every answer to start the conversation. You do need to distinguish the start of a discussion from approval of a final product. Use CCF's [official OEM/ODM process page](https://ccfrp.com/grow/oem-odm/) to discuss your references and application. Project feasibility, costs, timing and responsibilities must be confirmed for the actual scope.

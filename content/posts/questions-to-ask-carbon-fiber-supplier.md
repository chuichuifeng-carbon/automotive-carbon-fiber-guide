# 7 Questions to Ask a Carbon Fiber Supplier Before Placing an Order

Every B2B buyer has had the experience: you send an RFQ, get eight replies, pick the one with the best price, place an order, and then — six weeks later — the parts arrive warped, the mounting holes are wrong, and the supplier stops answering your emails.

The problem is not that good suppliers do not exist. It is that **most buyers ask the wrong questions**. "What is your price?" and "What is your MOQ?" matter, but they are commodity questions that every supplier will answer identically. The questions that separate a reliable factory from a trading middleman or a low-quality operation are the ones that force specificity — questions that cannot be answered with a copy-paste template.

This guide gives you seven questions to ask every carbon fiber supplier, ordered by how early in the conversation you should ask them. Each one includes:

- **Why it matters** — the specific risk it protects you from
- **What a good answer sounds like** — the benchmarks you should expect
- **Red flags to watch for** — answers that should make you walk away
- **A script you can copy-paste** — for your next Alibaba inquiry

## Question 1: "Can You Provide Your Business License and Factory Address — and Will You Do a Video Walkthrough?"

### Why it matters

An estimated **30–40% of carbon fiber suppliers on Alibaba and Made-in-China are trading companies**, not factories. They source parts from multiple sub-contractors, mark them up 15–30%, and have zero control over production quality, lead times, or defect rates. Worse, some are outright scammers who will take your deposit and disappear.

A genuine factory will have:
- A valid Chinese business license (Unified Social Credit Code) that matches the company name on their quotation
- A physical factory address (not a residential building or co-working space)
- Willingness to do a 5–10 minute live video walkthrough of the production floor, autoclave room, and QC station

Trading companies will:
- Stall on providing a license ("our manager is out of office")
- Give a vague address ("the factory is in Ningbo")
- Refuse video walks ("policy does not allow cameras on the floor")

### Good answer

> "Here is our business license PDF — company name matches our email signature. Our factory is at No. 37, Haishu District, Ningbo. I can do a WhatsApp video walkthrough tomorrow at 10 AM China time — I'll show you the lay-up station, the autoclave, and our QC inspector measuring parts with a coordinate measuring machine."

### Red flags

- Cannot produce a license after 3 requests
- Address is a registered office address (not a factory)
- "Factory walkthrough not possible due to company policy"

### Script

> Hi [name], before we proceed with samples, could you share your business license and confirm the factory address? Would you also be available for a 10-minute video walkthrough of your production facility this week? I'd like to see the autoclave and QC area.

## Question 2: "What Is Your Mold Manufacturing Process — and How Do You Ensure Accuracy?"

### Why it matters

Fitment problems trace directly to mold accuracy. A mold produced from a 3D scan of an OEM bumper, machined on a 5-axis CNC, and checked against a CMM (coordinate measuring machine) will produce parts within ±1 mm tolerance at mounting points. A mold that is a hand-laid copy of a copy — which is what budget factories use — accumulates 2–3 mm of distortion per copy generation, and by the third generation the mold no longer matches the car at all.

### Good answer

> "All our molds are produced from 3D scans of OEM vehicles. We use a 5-axis CNC to machine the mold tooling to ±0.05 mm precision, then verify every mold with a CMM before first article. Our molds are first-generation only — we never copy copies."

Before funding any new mold, clarify who owns the tooling, what the cost scope covers, and how changes are handled — these terms decide whether you can move production later and what a revision costs. ccfrp.com's [carbon fiber tooling and mold ownership questions](https://ccfrp.com/carbon-fiber-tooling-mold-ownership-questions/) list exactly what to pin down.

### Red flags

> "Our molds are hand-made by experienced workers" (no CNC, no scan data)
> "The mold is from our partner factory" (they do not control the mold)
> "Copy generation? We don't track that" (they have no mold lineage records)

### Follow-up question for depth

> "Can you show me a CMM report from your last mold for this exact part? I'd like to see the mounting point measurement tolerance."

### Script

> Thanks for the sample photos. Can you walk me through your mold manufacturing process for this part? Specifically: do you use OEM scan data, 5-axis CNC, and CMM verification? And is this a first-generation mold or a copy?

## Question 3: "Do You Test-Fit Every First Article on a Real Vehicle Before Production?"

### Why it matters

This single question eliminates the largest single risk in carbon fiber procurement: shipping an entire production batch of unverified geometry. A responsible factory produces one first article from every new mold, mounts it on an actual vehicle (or a verified production buck), measures every gap and mounting point, and only approves the mold for production if fitment passes their internal standard. Budget factories skip this step entirely — they rely on the mold being "close enough" and ship the first article *to you* as a "sample," hoping you will not test it rigorously.

### Good answer

> "Every new mold gets a first article test-fit on a vehicle in our verification bay. We measure gaps at 12 points with a feeler gauge, check every mounting hole against factory points, and photograph the fitment from 4 angles. If any gap exceeds 2 mm, we rework the mold before producing the batch."

A first-article fitment session is too important to leave to a verbal summary. Define the questions, owner agreement, personnel, photographs, and stop/release conditions in advance. ccfrp.com's guide on [planning a test-fit vehicle session](https://ccfrp.com/carbon-fiber-prototype-test-fit-vehicle/) gives the preparation checklist.

### Red flags

> "We don't have a vehicle for testing — you can test the sample on your car" (no verification)
> "Our workers check the fit by eye" (no measurement standard)
> "The sample we sent you *is* the first article — let us know how it fits" (they are using you as their QC department)

### Script

> Before we confirm the order, what is your first article verification process for new molds? Do you test-fit on a real vehicle, and if so, what measurement standard do you use to approve the mold for production?

## Question 4: "What Is Your AQL Inspection Standard — and Will You Allow a Third-Party SGS/TÜV Inspection Before Shipment?"

### Why it matters

In-process QC and pre-shipment inspection are two different things. A factory that says "we check every part" may mean a worker glances at each piece, or it may mean a QC inspector uses a CMM and a feeler gauge against a written standard. You do not know until you ask — and you do not know they are telling the truth until you bring in a third party.

**AQL (Acceptance Quality Limit)** is the industry standard for statistical sampling. AQL 2.5 means that for every 100 parts, 2–3 defective units are the acceptable upper limit. If the factory cannot state their AQL or refuses third-party inspection, assume their actual defect rate is 5–15% (typical for unregulated suppliers).

### Good answer

> "Our in-process QC uses AQL 2.5 at both lay-up and post-finish stages. We allow third-party SGS/TÜV inspection before shipment — we will reserve the production line on the inspection day and the inspector will have full access. The inspection fee is typically $250–$400 which we can split 50/50 or include in the unit price if you prefer."

If the sample is also a first article from a new mold, approve it against a documented revision and an explicit review scope before authorizing production. ccfrp.com's [prototype approval sign-off checklist](https://ccfrp.com/carbon-fiber-prototype-approval-checklist/) records accepted features, open issues, and exactly which next stage is authorized.

### Red flags

> "We do our own inspection — no SGS needed" (they want to self-certify)
> "Third-party inspection adds 2 weeks and $1,000" (exaggerated cost and lead time to discourage you)
> "AQL? What is that?" (no quality standard at all)

### Script

> What is your in-process QC sampling standard — do you use AQL? And to protect both of us, are you open to a third-party SGS/TÜV inspection 1–2 days before shipment? We can split the inspection fee.

## Question 5: "What Are Your Payment Terms — and Do You Accept a 30% Deposit + 70% Before Shipment?"

### Why it matters

Payment terms are the clearest signal of a supplier's confidence and risk posture. Here is the spectrum:

| Terms | What it signals | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 100% upfront | No confidence in production quality or cash flow; may be a scam | Extremely high |
| 50% deposit + 50% before shipment | Some risk; may be a smaller operation with limited cash | Medium |
| **30% deposit + 70% before shipment** | Standard, responsible factory with stable production | Low |
| Letter of Credit (LC) | Large established factory; willing to bear bank fees | Very low |
| Cash on delivery | Almost never happens in international trade | Too good to be true |

Any supplier demanding 100% upfront should be eliminated immediately. Any supplier refusing to accept 30/70 split is either financially unstable or has something to hide.

### Good answer

> "Our standard terms are 30% deposit by SWIFT wire, 70% balance paid before shipment — we will send production photos and QC results for your confirmation before you release the final payment. For orders over $50,000 we also accept irrevocable L/C."

### Red flags

> "We need 100% upfront — new customer policy" (extreme risk)
> "Pay 50% deposit, 50% after you receive the goods" (they do not trust their own product, or they cannot pay their suppliers without your money)
> "Only Western Union" (no business account, not a real factory)

### Script

> For this order, what are your standard payment terms? Our company policy is 30% deposit, 70% before shipment — is that something you can accommodate?

## Question 6: "What Is Your Shipping Packaging — and Do You Guarantee No Clear Coat Fogging in Container Transit?"

### Why it matters

Sea containers reach 60°C with 80%+ humidity during trans-Pacific voyages. Carbon fiber clear coat — especially lower-quality 2-pack polyurethanes — fogs under prolonged heat and moisture. This is not a cosmetic issue: it makes the part unsellable. And unlike a scratch or pinhole, fogging happens *after* inspection — so SGS will not catch it.

The fix is straightforward: desiccant packs inside every carton plus a moisture barrier bag wrapping each outer box. But budget factories skip both to save $0.10 per carton.

### Good answer

> "Every carton gets two 50g desiccant packs and is wrapped in a PE moisture barrier bag before palletization. Our warranty covers clear coat fogging during sea transit — if you receive fogged parts, we replace them or credit you 100%."

### Red flags

> "We just put the parts in cartons — never had fogging complaints" (no desiccant, no barrier)
> "Clear coat fogging is a shipping issue, not our responsibility" (they know it happens and will not stand behind it)
> "Desiccant? What is that?" (no transit damage awareness)

### Script

> We're concerned about clear coat fogging during sea transit. Can you describe your packaging and moisture protection? And do you provide a warranty against clear coat damage during shipping?

For private-label orders, packaging approval is a separate step from product approval — separate branding location, artwork revision, and packaging content from the engineering sign-off. ccfrp.com's [branding and packaging approval checklist](https://ccfrp.com/private-label-carbon-fiber-packaging-approval/) and [logo and badge placement guide](https://ccfrp.com/private-label-carbon-fiber-logo-placement/) keep these steps from being conflated.

## Question 7: "What Is Your Defective Part Replacement Policy — and How Fast Do You Process Claims?"

### Why it matters

Every factory ships defective parts occasionally. The difference between a good supplier and a bad one is how they handle it. A responsible factory will replace defective parts at their cost (including return freight) within 14–21 days, no questions asked. A bad factory will delay, argue, offer partial credits, or stop responding entirely.

This question is particularly important because defect replacement is one of the most common after-sales friction points — and the one most likely to destroy your margin if you have to absorb the cost yourself.

### Good answer

> "Our warranty covers any manufacturing defect within 30 days of you receiving the goods. You send us photos of the defect, we approve the claim within 48 hours, and we ship replacements or issue a 100% credit within 2 weeks. For larger claims, we will also cover return freight."

### Red flags

> "No warranty — 'sold as is'" (no accountability)
> "Warranty is 7 days after shipment date (not after you receive it)" (unrealistic — your goods are still in transit)
> "We will look at it but replacement depends on the situation" (no clear policy, expect disputes)
> "All sales final" (walk away immediately)

### Script

> For defective manufacturing parts, what is your replacement or credit policy? Specifically: how long is the warranty, how fast do you process claims, and do you cover return freight?

## FAQ

### Why these 7 questions and not 10 or 20?

These seven are the highest-signal questions in carbon fiber sourcing. Each one filters for a specific category of risk: identity verification, mold accuracy, fitment control, quality standards, payment security, transit protection, and after-sales accountability. Asking more questions does not add signal — it just delays your sourcing cycle. Asking fewer means you leave one category of risk uncovered.

### What if a supplier refuses the third-party inspection?

Eliminate. A factory that will not let an independent inspector look at their production has something to hide — typically a high defect rate or a mold accuracy problem they do not want documented. The $250–$400 inspection fee is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy in this business.

### Is a 50/50 payment split really a red flag?

Not always. A smaller or newer factory may not have the cash flow to cover material costs without 50% upfront, and they genuinely do not have scam intent. But the risk profile is higher — you have more money on the line before you see any production output. If you are working with a supplier who only offers 50/50, do additional verification: ask for more factory photos, get a reference from another buyer, and make sure the 70% balance is conditional on inspection photos (not just "before shipment").

### How long should I wait for answers to these questions?

A serious factory will respond to all seven within 48 hours. Trading companies may take longer as they forward your questions to their sub-contractors. If you get no reply after 3 business days, move on — they are either too busy with bigger clients or not serious about your business.

### Can I skip some questions for repeat suppliers?

Once you have successfully received 2–3 shipments with a supplier, you can relax some questions — but not all. Questions 5 (payment terms) and 7 (defective part policy) are contractual and should be re-confirmed for every order. Questions 2 and 3 (mold and first article) should be asked for *any new part number* even from a trusted supplier. Questions 1 and 4 can be verified once and assumed valid unless the supplier changes ownership or location.

### What about sample requests — do these questions apply there too?

Absolutely. The sample is your first test of the supplier's process. If a supplier gives you vague or evasive answers to these questions *before* you even order a sample, their production quality will not improve once you place a larger order. Ask all seven before committing to sample payment — it will save you $40–$120 in courier fees on suppliers you would have eliminated anyway.

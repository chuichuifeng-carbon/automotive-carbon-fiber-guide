# Dry Carbon vs Wet Carbon: Performance, Weight & Fitment Compared

Every serious B2B carbon fiber buyer asks this question sooner or later: **dry carbon or wet carbon?**

The answer is not "dry is better" or "wet is cheaper." It depends entirely on what you are making, who is buying it, and what they care about most. A street-driven car owner may never notice the difference between a dry carbon mirror cap and a wet carbon one. A track racer replacing a rear wing will absolutely notice — and care.

This guide breaks down exactly what dry and wet carbon are, how they are manufactured side by side, where the real differences show up (weight, strength, fitment, surface), and which one you should specify for each product category. By the end you will have a clear specification you can send to suppliers instead of guessing from marketing terms.

## What Exactly Is Dry Carbon?

**Dry carbon** — also called **pre-preg carbon** or **autoclave carbon** — is made from carbon fiber fabric that has already been impregnated with epoxy resin and then frozen. The fabric comes to the factory as a rolled sheet of "pre-preg" (pre-impregnated) material, kept cold to prevent the resin from curing prematurely.

The manufacturing process for a dry carbon part:

1. Pre-preg rolls are cut into precise patterns using CNC cutting machines.
2. A worker lays the pre-preg plies into a polished mold by hand, following a ply book that specifies fiber direction for each layer.
3. The mold is placed into a vacuum bag and sealed.
4. The entire assembly goes into an **autoclave** — a large pressure vessel — where it is heated to 120–140°C under 6–7 bar of pressure for 1.5–3 hours.
5. The heat cures the epoxy, and the pressure squeezes out voids, resulting in a part with extremely consistent fiber content (typically 58–62% by weight).

The word "dry" refers to the fact that the resin is already in the fabric before lay-up — the worker is laying "dry-looking" pre-preg sheets, not painting or infusing wet resin.

## What Exactly Is Wet Carbon?

**Wet carbon** — or **hand lay-up carbon** — is made from dry carbon fiber fabric that gets resin applied at the factory during manufacturing. The resin is not pre-impregnated; it is poured, brushed, or infused onto the fabric at room temperature or with mild heat.

There are two main wet carbon processes:

### Hand lay-up

1. Dry carbon fabric is cut to pattern.
2. A worker paints liquid epoxy onto both the mold surface and each fabric ply.
3. Plies are laid up by hand, one at a time, with resin brushed between each layer.
4. The mold is typically vacuum bagged (but not autoclaved) and cured at room temperature or up to 60°C.
5. Result: fiber content of 45–55% by weight, lower than dry carbon.

### Vacuum infusion

1. Dry fabric is laid into a sealed mold cavity.
2. Liquid resin is pulled into the mold through vacuum pressure, saturating the fabric evenly.
3. Curing at room temperature or mild heat.
4. Result: fiber content of 50–58%, better consistency than hand lay-up, still below pre-preg.

The word "wet" simply means the fabric is dry when laid and resin is added afterward.

## Side-by-Side Comparison Table

This table is the most useful single reference you can have when sourcing. Every row affects your product's value proposition, your warranty claims, and your customer reviews.

| Property | Dry Carbon (Pre-Preg Autoclave) | Wet Carbon (Hand Lay-Up / Vacuum Infusion) | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiber content by weight | 58–62% | 45–58% | Dry |
| Part weight for same geometry | Baseline | +15–25% heavier | Dry |
| Specific tensile strength | 3,500–4,500 MPa | 2,500–3,500 MPa | Dry |
| Stiffness / modulus | 230–270 GPa | 180–240 GPa | Dry |
| Void content (internal porosity) | < 1% | 2–8% | Dry |
| Fitment tolerance | ±0.5 mm typical | ±1.5–3 mm typical | Dry |
| Surface finish | Pinhole-free, uniform weave | Can show resin-rich areas, slight weave distortion | Dry (but wet can match with clear coat) |
| UV stability | Excellent (epoxy cured under pressure = fully crosslinked) | Good with clear coat, poorer without | Dry |
| Tooling cost | Higher (autoclave + polished molds) | Lower (no autoclave, simpler molds) | Wet |
| MOQ | Higher (autoclave batch economics) | Lower (small runs possible) | Wet |
| Unit price | +30–60% vs wet for same part | Baseline | Wet |

The most important takeaway from this table is not that dry carbon wins every column — it is that **the premium you pay for dry carbon buys you measurable improvements in weight, strength, fitment, and consistency**. Whether those improvements matter depends entirely on your customer's use case.

"The dry carbon version is how much lighter?" is the second question after "is it actually stronger?" Here are real weight comparisons we measure on identical geometry:

| Part | Wet carbon weight | Dry carbon weight | Savings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mirror cap (pair) | 280 g | 225 g | 20% |
| Rear spoiler (600 mm) | 1,250 g | 980 g | 22% |

| Side skirt (pair) | 2,400 g | 1,850 g | 23% |
| Rear diffuser | 2,100 g | 1,650 g | 21% |

The savings is remarkably consistent: **20–23% lighter across all exterior trim and body parts**. The reason is simple — higher fiber content means less resin, and carbon fiber is 3× stiffer than cured epoxy per unit weight.

For street cars, 22% less weight on a mirror cap is irrelevant (55 grams). For race cars chasing marginal gains, every gram matters — and dry carbon delivers that predictably across every part.

## Fitment Accuracy: Why Dry Carbon Fits Better

Fitment is where the dry vs wet carbon difference becomes visible to the end user. A bad-fitting part creates the single largest source of aftermarket carbon fiber complaints and returns.

Why does dry carbon fit better?

**1. Polished steel molds.** Dry carbon parts are pressed against highly polished, temperature-stabilized steel molds in the autoclave. The pressure forces every ply against the mold surface, replicating the mold geometry with ±0.5 mm accuracy. Wet carbon molds are often fiberglass or composite, and the hand lay-up or infusion process introduces dimensional variation from ply-to-ply.

**2. Vacuum bag pressure consistency.** Autoclave pressure (6–7 bar) is uniform across every square millimeter of the part. Wet carbon vacuum bagging typically achieves 0.8–1 bar, and pressure can vary across the mold surface depending on bag wrinkles and breather material placement. Uneven pressure = uneven thickness = dimensional drift.

**3. Lower resin content = less shrinkage.** When epoxy cures it shrinks roughly 2–3%. Higher fiber content (dry carbon at 60% vs wet at 50%) means less resin to shrink. The result: dry carbon parts hold their post-cure dimensions far more closely than wet carbon parts.

For B2B buyers, this translates directly into installation time. A dry carbon front lip may bolt straight on in 15 minutes. A wet carbon lip may need 1–3 hours of body shop trimming, filing, and gap adjustment. When the end buyer pays a body shop $80–$150 per hour, that "cheaper" wet carbon part becomes significantly more expensive to install.

## How to Choose: Street, Track, Interior, Structural

The right choice depends on what the part is, who is buying it, and what they will do with it. Here is our buyer's decision framework:

| Use case | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Street car exterior trim (mirrors, handles, key covers) | **Wet carbon** | Weight savings is negligible; surface finish matters more than marginal stiffness; price-sensitive market |
| Street car body parts (spoilers, lips, diffusers) | **Wet carbon if fitment is proven; dry if it isn't** | Check supplier's fitment tolerance first — if they guarantee ±1 mm, wet is fine for street |
| Track / motorsport body parts | **Dry carbon** | Every gram counts; structural stiffness matters at high speed; failure is a safety risk |
| Interior trim (dash inserts, steering wheel overlays, door panels) | **Wet carbon** | Hidden from UV; fitment on interior panels is usually hand-fitted anyway; aesthetics outweigh weight |
| Structural replacement panels (roofs, quarter panels, rear hatchbacks) | **Dry carbon — always** | These carry crash and load requirements; void content and tensile strength matter for safety |
| Private label / e-commerce SKUs | **Wet carbon for entry-level, dry for premium tier** | Two-SKU strategy lets you capture both price-sensitive and enthusiast buyers |

A common mistake: specifying dry carbon for every part because you heard it is "better." That overbuilds most street-driven applications and kills your margin. A mirror cap in wet carbon at $85 and dry carbon at $135 — both look identical — means the wet one sells 3× faster on Amazon.

## How to Tell Dry From Wet When Inspecting a Sample

If a supplier tells you a part is dry carbon, verify it physically. Do not rely on marketing language alone.

**Three field checks:**

1. **Weave definition.** Lay it flat under bright overhead light and look straight down at it. Dry carbon has crisp, sharply defined weave lines — every fiber looks exactly in place. Wet carbon can show slightly blurry or wavy weave patterns where resin has pooled between plies or where hand lay-up shifted the fabric.

2. **Edge cross-section.** Find a raw or unfinished edge (many parts have a factory flange that is not painted). Dry carbon shows tight, uniform ply layers with no visible gaps or bubbles. Wet carbon may show small voids (pinholes) between layers or resin-rich areas that look like thicker, darker lines.

3. **Weight comparison.** If you have a known dry carbon sample of similar geometry, weigh both. A wet carbon part will be visibly heavier — 15–25% more mass for the same external size.

## Common Myths Debunked

**Myth 1: "Dry carbon is always carbon fiber and wet carbon is fiberglass."** False. Both use the same carbon fiber fabric. The difference is in how the resin is applied and cured.

**Myth 2: "Wet carbon is low quality."** False. Vacuum infusion wet carbon can produce very consistent parts with excellent fitment. The real difference is *tolerance*, not quality. A well-engineered wet carbon part will outperform a badly made dry carbon part every time.

**Myth 3: "Dry carbon cannot be repaired."** False. Both dry and wet carbon can be repaired with standard epoxy and sanding. The repair process is identical because the resin matrix is fundamentally the same (epoxy).

**Myth 4: "Dry carbon is always more expensive per kilogram."** Not in all cases. Dry carbon's raw material (pre-preg) is more expensive, but its higher fiber content means you get more carbon fiber per kilogram of finished part. For structural applications, the effective cost per unit stiffness favors dry carbon.

## FAQ

### Does dry carbon always require an autoclave?

For the performance benefits described in this guide, yes. You can cure pre-preg with a convection oven (called "out-of-autoclave" or OOA prepreg), but the pressure is limited to vacuum only, so void content is higher (3–5% vs <1% for autoclave). OOA prepreg is a middle ground — better than wet carbon but not true autoclave dry carbon.

### Can wet carbon be made to dry carbon specifications?

No. The fiber content ceiling for vacuum infusion is about 58%, just below the dry carbon minimum of 58%. To get higher fiber content and eliminate voids, you need pressure — which means an autoclave. That is not a wet carbon process anymore.

### Is the clear coat on dry carbon different?

Typically no. Both dry and wet carbon parts receive the same 2K clear coat system for UV protection and surface gloss. The difference is that dry carbon's epoxy is already fully crosslinked in the autoclave, so the clear coat bonds more reliably and UV degradation of the underlying resin is much slower.

### What about prepreg that is hand laid and vacuum bagged without autoclave?

As noted above, this is out-of-autoclave (OOA) prepreg, not true dry carbon. It offers better fiber content than wet carbon (~55–58%) but higher void content than autoclave dry carbon (~3–5%). It is a cost-saving middle ground for applications where full autoclave performance is not required.

### How much more MOQ does dry carbon require?

Typically 50–100% higher MOQ. The autoclave has a minimum batch size for economical operation — running 2–3 pieces of dry carbon in a $300 autoclave cycle is wasteful, so factories prefer batches of 10–20. Wet carbon has no autoclave bottleneck and can run 3–5 pieces economically.

### Should I specify dry carbon for e-commerce private label?

For premium-priced listings ($150+ on Amazon), yes — the "dry carbon autoclave" badge is a genuine selling point that commands higher margins at enthusiast price points. For entry-level listings ($60–$100), stick with wet carbon and focus on fitment consistency instead of material marketing.

A dry-carbon front lip costs more than a wet-carbon alternative. Should your shop pay the difference? Only after finding out what the two offers actually contain. One may be a prepreg carbon assembly; another may combine a carbon face with different backing. They may also have different mounting parts, finishes or supplied sections.

**Choose the construction that meets the product's requirements and your customer's purchase, then compare complete delivered assemblies.** Dry carbon is not a universal performance grade. Wet carbon is not a synonym for fake carbon. Both labels need a more precise manufacturing description before they can support a wholesale decision.

## What dry carbon and wet carbon usually mean

In automotive aftermarket listings, dry carbon commonly refers to prepreg-based construction. Prepreg reinforcement already contains its resin system before the manufacturer lays it into the tool. The material still needs the specified storage, handling, compaction and cure process.

Wet layup introduces liquid resin to the reinforcement during manufacture. Vacuum bagging may be used as part of the process. Resin infusion is another route: resin is drawn into dry reinforcement under vacuum. Ask suppliers to name the route instead of grouping every non-prepreg part under one loose label.

Prepreg does not automatically mean autoclave cured. Easy Composites publishes an [out-of-autoclave component prepreg processing guide](https://media.easycomposites.co.uk/datasheets/EC-TDS-XC110-Processing-Guide.pdf), a concrete example of a material designed for a different cure route. That document describes its own system, not the material or process used in any CCF product.

| Description | What it identifies | What still needs product evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Prepreg / dry carbon | Reinforcement supplied with resin already incorporated | Cure route, laminate, finished mass and application |
| Wet layup | Liquid resin introduced during layup | Compaction, cure, backing and finished quality |
| Resin infusion | Resin introduced through reinforcement under vacuum | Material system, process control and acceptance results |
| Full carbon or hybrid | A claim about construction | Which layers and supporting elements the claim includes |
| Gloss or matte | Surface appearance | Manufacturing process and coating performance |

## “Real carbon” does not describe the entire part

A visible carbon face can be combined with fiberglass backing, a bonded carrier, inserts or separate brackets. Those details affect the comparison. Ask what each supplier includes in the construction statement and which components are separate.

A hybrid product can contain genuine carbon fiber. The purchasing problem arises when the buyer expects an all-carbon laminate and receives an undisclosed different construction. Equally, describing every hidden bracket as carbon would be misleading where the assembly uses another material.

Request the exact SKU's specification, rear views and edge details. Photographs help identify questions but cannot reliably reveal every layer. For a new engineered product, the agreed design and verification record should establish the construction; a glossy sales image cannot do that job.

CCF's [product directory](https://ccfrp.com/products/) helps identify the component you want quoted. Confirm its individual construction instead of applying one process description to the entire range.

## Weight: compare the same finished purchase

A lighter part can matter to a buyer, but only a like-for-like measurement establishes the difference. Compare a complete lip with a complete lip, or a replacement panel assembly with the equivalent assembly. Record whether brackets, carriers, fasteners and coatings are included.

A resin-rich or thicker construction can affect mass, but there is no universal percentage saving that follows from the words dry and wet. Geometry, reinforcement, backing and the finished assembly all matter. Ask for measured mass and its scope rather than publishing a generic comparison chart.

An overlay also adds a part over an existing surface. Its weight should not be presented as a saving against a removed component when nothing is removed. ## For a front lip, mounting and clearance can decide the purchase

A premium process label will not correct a mismatched bumper interface. Review the underside seating surface, supplied sections and mounting method before accepting either sample. Include the customer's actual bumper and any previous modifications in that review.

For a segmented lip, inspect joint alignment and the appearance between pieces. For a full-width part, inspect seating across the whole span. Neither construction justifies using fasteners to pull a poorly locating component into place.

Consider the complete purchase cost: goods, required installation materials, packing, freight and labor. A lower-priced lip with a larger carton and separately sourced mounting pieces may have a different delivered margin than its headline price suggests. The [front-lip buying comparison]({{< relref "posts/carbon-fiber-quote-hardware-scope.md" >}}) shows how to separate those items.

For existing products, request the supported application alongside the material description through [CCF wholesale](https://ccfrp.com/grow/wholesale/). Process and fitment are two questions; the quotation should answer both.

## For a hood or wing, the design requirement comes first

Replacement panels and supported wing assemblies require more than a cosmetic comparison. Latches, hinges, supports, mounting interfaces and intended service conditions belong in the specification. The relevant engineer and installer need to assess the complete product.

Do not infer load capability from visible weave, stiffness by hand or an autoclave photograph. A stronger-looking mounting foot is not a tested assembly, and a stationary fitment check does not establish aerodynamic performance.

If a custom part has mass, stiffness, temperature or other performance requirements, define them before selecting the manufacturing route. Use [CCF OEM/ODM review](https://ccfrp.com/grow/oem-odm/) to discuss the design inputs, proposed construction and necessary verification separately from a catalog price inquiry.

## Gloss, matte and woven appearance will not identify the process

Both prepreg and non-prepreg products can have a glossy or matte finish. A visible twill pattern identifies appearance, not cure pressure or the complete laminate. A chopped-fiber appearance likewise needs its own material and process description.

For a reseller, the practical appearance test is the delivered sample. Inspect exposed edges, corner transitions, the main visible face and the attachment side. Compare a pair together under consistent lighting. If the part must match existing trim, use an actual reference rather than promising a match from the weave name.

CCF's [forged-versus-woven discussion](https://ccfrp.com/forged-vs-woven-carbon-fiber-car-parts/) addresses the appearance choice. Its [gloss-versus-matte guide](https://ccfrp.com/gloss-vs-matte-carbon-fiber-car-parts/) addresses the finish. Keep both separate from the process claim on your product page.

Coating durability is another specification. Dry carbon does not automatically mean scratch-proof or permanently resistant to discoloration. Ask for relevant coating information and evidence when those properties form part of the customer's requirements.

## Is the dry-carbon premium commercially useful?

For a customer who values a verified construction or measured mass target, a suitably documented prepreg product may justify a higher price. For a cosmetic purchase, another construction may meet the appearance, fitment and cost requirements. Evaluate the actual offers instead of assigning quality tiers from their names.

Ask whether your sales team can explain the premium accurately. If the only difference you can demonstrate is a supplier's label, you have little basis for promising a performance benefit. If you have documented construction, a measured comparison and a properly evaluated sample, the customer has something concrete to judge.

Also compare repeat supply. A well-described sample is useful only when the next order remains identifiable. Preserve the accepted SKU, revision, finish and contents, and ask how changes will be communicated.

## What to request before ordering samples

Send the same requirements to each supplier: vehicle application, exact product, desired finish, quantity and destination. Ask for the manufacturing route, construction including backing, complete contents, measured mass where relevant and installation information.

Then use [CCF's sample inspection guide](https://ccfrp.com/carbon-fiber-sample-inspection-wholesale-order/) to plan a product-specific review. Record appearance, contents and physical fit before permanent alterations. A single attractive sample cannot establish an untested structural or durability claim.

If the two quotes still cover different products, keep them separate. First decide which construction and installation scope serve the customer; only then calculate which supplier offers better value for that purchase.

## Buyer questions

### Is wet carbon fake carbon?

Not inherently. Wet layup can use genuine carbon reinforcement. Establish the actual laminate and any backing; a carbon-look printed surface is a different product description.

### Does dry carbon always require an autoclave?

No. Prepreg systems exist for out-of-autoclave processing. The selected material's specified process and the manufacturer's actual route need to agree.

### Is dry carbon always lighter?

The label alone cannot establish that. Compare equivalent finished products with a documented weighing scope.

### Which should a tuning shop stock?

Stock products with verified applications, evaluated samples and a construction your team can describe honestly. Where both routes meet the customer requirement, compare the complete delivered cost and support terms rather than the name alone.
